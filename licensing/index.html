    Licensing¶

    Source-available under the PolyForm Noncommercial License 1.0.0: free for personal, hobby and other noncommercial use, including reading, modifying and sharing the source. Commercial use requires a paid licence.

    Commits made before the licence changed remain under GPLv3, the licence they were published under.

    What is free, permanently¶

    Everything a single repository needs to be worked in day to day: staging, committing, discarding, history, blame, diffing, branching, merging, transport, conflict resolution, stashing — and the interactive rebase, which is what "worked in day to day" means on any repository with a review culture, and which is the single most conspicuous thing GitKraken charges for.

    Named, because "day to day" is doing a lot of work in that sentence:

    Free Which is
    Changes and staging File, hunk and line, staging and discarding alike, plus ignore and delete
    Reading a diff Unified, split, syntax, word marks, images, binary, folds
    History, graph and search The walk, the lanes, six search scopes, the filters
    Blame and file history Including renames and the change heatmap
    Comparing two refs All three readings
    Branches Create, rename, delete, check out, upstreams, drift, the table
    Rewriting history The interactive rebase, amend, reword, edit, cherry-pick, revert, reset
    Merging and conflicts Unconditionally — the whole Conflicts surface, plus your own merge tool
    Tags and notes Both kinds of tag, and a commit's note
    Stash Create, apply, pop and drop
    Worktrees and submodules The whole lifecycle of each
    Sparse checkout The cone-mode verbs
    Remotes and transport Fetch, push, pull, clone, the lease, the credential ladder

    And one row in that table is marked free rather than paid, so the line is visible and one word from moving: rebase-interactive, the interactive rebase.

    The list in the app is generated from the same table that does the gating, so it cannot come to disagree with what is actually gated. Moving a feature between the tiers is one word in one row, and that row also carries the words the offer is written from — a feature cannot be gated and left off the page that sells it, nor listed there while being free.

    Six promises a lapsed licence keeps¶

    These are the invariants that stop a pricing decision becoming data loss, and they are named tests rather than intentions:

    1. A gated control is absent, not greyed. It does not register, does not take a hint key, and does not appear in the palette.
    2. Exactly one discoverable route to what a licence adds — the licence window, reachable from the application menu and by name from the palette. Without it, absent controls would make the paid tier invisible and unsellable: the rule against greying is not a licence to hide the offer.
    3. Never gate an exit from a stopped operation. Continuing or aborting a rebase, a merge, a cherry-pick or a bisect, resolving a conflict, and writing a resolution are free for ever. A trial that lapses overnight must not leave anybody in a detached HEAD with no button. The check refuses those ids ahead of the feature table, rather than relying on them having been left out of it, because the table is the thing most likely to be edited in a hurry.
    4. The entire Conflicts surface is free, unconditionally. A merge run at a command line can drop somebody onto it.
    5. Stash create, apply, pop and drop are free. Stashing is where work goes to be safe.
    6. Downgrading is read-only, never destructive. Forty registered repositories still list and still open; only the forty-first is refused. Nothing deletes an entry to enforce a limit.

    A gated control is drawn, and pressing it opens the offer¶

    This reverses what the app used to do, and the old rule is worth stating because most of its reasoning survives: an absent control never lies about what this copy can do, and absent-not-greyed is how every other unavailable thing here behaves.

    What it costs is that somebody who has not paid sees no evidence the paid tier exists at all — and a feature nobody can see is one nobody asks about, so somebody who would happily pay never finds out what for.

    There is a way back: a setting hides the paid features, off by default and offered only on an unlicensed copy. It is stored and honoured on every copy all the same, so a licence that lapses does not silently un-hide what somebody put away.

    The distinction the two rules turn on: "may this be shown" and "may this be run" are separate questions, and the second goes on keeping paid commands out of both keyboard routes.

    How the check works¶

    Offline, and there is no phone-home. An Ed25519 signature over the key's payload, verified with a public key compiled into the binary.

    A key is read from an environment variable, then a file in the app's own configuration directory, then the settings — and which of the three answered is published, because "Remove" clears the two the app owns and cannot unset a variable in a way that would survive the next launch. The button is absent for the environment rung and the verb refuses by naming the variable if it is reached anyway.

    Coverage is measured against the build, not against the calendar. The price is one payment for the version bought, with bug fixes for a year and none after — so a licence covers any build committed within a year of its issue date, compared against the commit date of the build you are running. Compared against today it would lapse while you were still running the build you paid for. A build whose commit date is unknown (a tarball) is covered: a tarball is not evidence of anything, and refusing there punishes the one reader who cannot check.

    Two public keys, and rotation is why. Either is accepted, so retiring a compromised key means shipping a build that drops it and keeps the other — and everything the survivor signed goes on working. With one key that same event would invalidate every licence ever sold.

    What rotation does not do

    It does not revoke. A leaked key mints licences until a build omitting it reaches the reader, and no client-side check can do better. Rotation bounds the damage in time; it does not undo it.

    No obfuscation, no anti-tamper, no phone-home. A check that runs on your own machine is bypassable by anybody who wants to bypass it. The goal is a clear offer and an honest refusal.

    The check knows nothing about this app¶

    Everything about checking a licence — the key format, the signature, the three places a key may be kept, coverage against the build date, and which features an id is allowed — is its own library, linking Qt Core and libcrypto and not the rest of the application. A file there that reached for a repository, a preference or one of this app's commands would not compile.

    That is what makes "it knows nothing about this application" a fact the build enforces rather than a claim in a comment. What a product is — the key prefix, the public keys, the environment variable, the file name, the coverage window, the feature table and the ids that may never be gated — is data, and this app's is nine lines.

    Three things stayed with the app because none could be anything else's: the table, because moving a feature between tiers is one word in one row; the ungatable set, because which verbs are a way out of something this app started is a fact about this app; and the fleet cap, which is the one thing the package refuses to model — "how many of the host's own things" can only be answered in some product's own vocabulary.

    The library's own test drives it as a different product would, inventing one with its own prefix, variable, file, settings entry, coverage window and two features. That is the only thing that can say the package is reusable.

    The build with no paid tier on screen¶

    cmake -B build -DSOURCEMANAGER_FREE_ONLY=ON .

    Hides the paid features, the switch that would show them, and the licence window — for handing a copy to somebody who should not be offered something they cannot buy.

    It is not a second product: the gate, the feature table and the licence check compile in exactly as they do otherwise, and only what is drawn changes. The suite passes in both configurations.

    Third-party software¶

    The app links Qt, libgit2 and OpenSSL, and ships SQLite inside Qt's SQL driver. Every one of those is dynamically linked, unmodified, and named in the app under About → Third-party notices, with the verbatim licence texts, the copyright lines, and where the corresponding source can be obtained.

    That screen is generated rather than written out, so the versions in it are the ones actually loaded rather than the ones this was compiled against — which matters because the project ships against Homebrew's Qt on macOS, aqtinstall's on Windows and the distribution's on Linux. A notice naming "Qt 6.8.0" is a claim about which Qt you are entitled to the source of, and a hardcoded string would be wrong on two platforms out of three.

    The texts live in the repository and travel inside the binary, and a test walks the rows against them in both directions — a dependency added without its licence text fails the suite rather than shipping a notice with a hole in it, and a text carried that nothing names is either a dropped dependency whose row survived or weight nobody dares remove.

    Each is checked against a phrase from its opening and one from its closing, and the pair is the point: the first catches a document that is not this licence, the second catches one that is and stops early — which no length check can honestly tell from a document that is legitimately short.

    Nothing is modified, and the notice says so rather than staying silent — so the day somebody patches a dependency, that test fails and the notice is corrected, which is the only moment anybody would think to.

    libgit2's linking exception travels with its GPL rather than beside it: read on its own, the GPLv2 text says the opposite of what applies, and that exception is the whole reason this app can be closed-source at all.

    OpenSSL's licence changed at 3.0, so the row reads the loaded major version and, on an older library, names the older licence and points at the project instead of claiming a text this build does not carry.

    The build tools — moc, rcc, uic, lrelease — are deliberately absent from the notices: they are GPLv3, which would matter if they were shipped. They are not; they run at build time under The Qt Company's GPL Exception, which exists to permit exactly this, and nothing of theirs is in the binary.

    If you redistribute a build

    Qt is used under the LGPLv3. Nothing here is statically linked, so it can be relinked against a Qt of your own — and the app says where the libraries are on each platform, because "you may relink" is not much use without saying where they are.

    That obligation travels with a redistributed build: keep the notices screen, keep the texts, and do not link Qt statically without a commercial Qt licence.

    Before shipping a macOS bundle, tools/verify-macos-bundle.sh reports anything it loads from outside itself — a Homebrew libgit2 that macdeployqt did not copy in works on the machine that built it and on no other.
