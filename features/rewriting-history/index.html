    Rewriting history¶

    Licensing — Free. The interactive rebase is free and is named in the table as such, as are amend, reword, edit, cherry-pick, revert and reset. What a licence adds

    Six verbs, all entered from a commit in History rather than from a menu, and all arranged around the same two rules: refuse rather than clobber, and a stopped operation is always visible with a way out.

    Interactive rebase¶

    Two shapes, and which one you get depends on where you started.

    Entered from an object: it applies¶

    "Rebase onto this", from a commit's menu, a branch row's ⋮, or a commit dropped on another commit. One call that plans as picks and replays at once.

    This used to plan and then navigate to the Rebase surface, which was the wrong reading of the bargain: somebody who has picked a base out of the graph has taken the decision, and what they were handed was a page asking them to take it again on a plan whose every row was a pick.

    Seven things about it are deliberate:

    • An empty plan is a fast-forward, not a refusal. A plan is the commits reachable from HEAD but not from the base, so an empty one means the branch is simply behind — and git rebase <upstream> fast-forwards there and reports success. Refusing was the app disagreeing with the very line it prints beside the control, under a sentence ("There is nothing to rebase") that was true about the plan and useless about the intention.
    • HEAD already being on the base is a no-op announced rather than refused, because git exits zero there and a correct no-op drawn in the failure colour is how a reader learns to distrust the toast.
    • Every commit is a pick, whatever the Rebase surface's mode chip says — that control is not on screen here, and a plan seeded by Autosquash would fold your fixup! commits because of a setting you cannot see.
    • Nothing is autostashed. A dirty working tree is refused in words that name what to do; stashing your working tree, untracked files included, because a row was pressed is not a default this route gets to take.
    • The plan is kept on that refusal, so the Rebase tab is one press away with the plan already loaded, and its Apply honours the autostash tick.
    • Nothing is navigated to, on either outcome. A replay that worked has rewritten the history the surface is drawing and the graph moving is the feedback; a replay that stopped is in the banner, on every surface.
    • There is no confirmation. It is entered from the object it acts on, the branch it rewrites is the one you are standing on, Abort is on the banner, and the reflog holds where HEAD was — which is also what the log row offers to put back. A dialog would buy a second reading of a decision already taken and cost the whole of what was asked for.

    The Rebase surface¶

    Where a plan is edited: reorder, squash, fixup, reword, drop, edit, and the autosquash mode. Plan first, see the resulting branch, then apply — and nothing is written until you do.

    Rows are dragged by a grip, and the gesture has two meanings because a row is dropped in two different ways: between two rows is a reorder, and onto one is a fold, which is what squash is. Thirds rather than halves — folding rewrites two commits into one, so it should take aim rather than be what a slightly-off drop does.

    The index arithmetic is the whole of what those two verbs are for: dragging a row down, its target shifts up by one as the row is lifted out; dragging up, it has not moved. Got wrong either way the commit is folded into its neighbour — a rebase that reads correctly and replays something nobody asked for.

    A fold is refused rather than quietly reordering for a commit dropped onto itself, an id that is not in the plan, and a target that is being dropped — folding into a commit that is not going to exist is not a thing to do quietly.

    How it actually runs¶

    The replay is driven by cherry-pick, not libgit2's rebase driver: that one builds its operation list as all-picks and offers no public way to substitute a todo list, so squash, fixup, reword, drop and reorder would all be impossible through it.

    Two consequences to know:

    • HEAD must end up attached. During the replay HEAD is detached and follows each new commit; finishing and aborting re-attach it.
    • The state is persisted, so quitting mid-rebase does not lose the operation, and the fleet card reports it.

    Commits the replay creates are signed if commit.gpgsign says so — which is a departure from git, argued on the record.

    Rewording and editing one commit¶

    Amending is HEAD's own verb and says so. These two are the rebase with the plan built for you instead of by you: plan onto the commit's own first parent, set that one row's action to reword or edit, and start — every other row a plain pick.

    What the two verbs add is that somebody fixing a typo four commits back does not have to know any of that.

    A reword finishes and an edit deliberately does not. A message is known before the operation starts, so the stop the replay makes for one is answered by the controller and you never see the rebase surface. Content is not known beforehand — the whole point of editing is to change it — so that one stops on the commit with its files on disk, and "Amend and continue" on the banner is what ends it. Which is why the reword has a dialog and the edit opens nothing.

    The cost is a sentence and not a warning. "3 commits get new hashes" is a fact you can act on where "this rewrites history" is a noise everybody clicks through. The one thing that is a warning is a published commit, said once and done anyway.

    Three refusals, each because the alternative is worse than a message:

    • A root commit has no parent to replay onto, and the refusal points at amending.
    • A commit not reachable from HEAD would produce a plan that does not contain it and a replay that silently rewrote the branch you are on, for nothing.
    • An empty message is refused rather than read as "keep what it has" — here the whole operation is the message, so an empty one replays the branch and rewrites every hash above the commit to change nothing.

    Both rows are absent on HEAD: amending is what changes that commit, it is one press away in the commit box, and a whole rebase to reword the tip would be the wrong tool offered beside the right one.

    Cherry-pick¶

    The same machinery, exposed as a verb of its own and entered from the commit.

    Two modes, and -n is the one that matters. Committing behaves like git cherry-pick; not committing behaves like git cherry-pick -n, laying the change into the index and the working tree and recording nothing. The second is the primary, because the app already has a surface for reading a pending change and committing it — so the pick lands in Changes to be reviewed before it is permanent, and an unwanted one is discarded the way any other uncommitted edit is.

    The two modes differ in exactly one argument and nowhere else, which is what keeps them from drifting apart.

    HEAD is never detached, so it cannot end up detached — the rebase has to detach because it is rebuilding the branch it is standing on, and a pick has no such need. There is no window in which a crash leaves the repository detached.

    A range is applied oldest first, stopping at the first conflict, and the order is resolved with a revwalk rather than trusting the caller — every list in this UI is newest-first, and a range applied that way conflicts with itself on the second commit.

    It refuses a dirty working tree (which is what makes abort safe: there is no work of your own for the reset to take with it), and refuses while any other sequencer is running, because two of them writing one index is how a half-applied operation happens. A commit that comes out empty against HEAD is named rather than recorded as a commit that says nothing.

    Revert¶

    Reverting means reverse-applying the change into the index and the working tree, committing nothing — git revert --no-commit for a whole commit, git apply --reverse --index for a file, a hunk or a set of lines. The undo is handed to Changes to be reviewed like any other edit, which also means an unwanted revert is discarded rather than needing a second commit.

    Both sides are written rather than the working tree alone, because libgit2 validates the working tree against the index before applying, and the two disagreeing would make the next revert of the same file fail for a reason you could not see.

    The mechanism is chosen per granularity rather than forced into one:

    Granularity How
    A whole commit A real three-way merge, which is what copes with a merge commit and with a commit whose changes have since been rewritten
    One file The commit's own diff with the trees handed over in the other order, so add, delete and rename come out right with no patch text
    A hunk or lines A reverse patch built by hand, since libgit2 cannot be asked for a subset of a diff's hunks; an unpicked change is carried through as context rather than dropped

    Every route refuses rather than clobbers. A conflicting whole-commit revert is rejected outright with the paths named, instead of writing markers into a working tree you may have unrelated work in — this operation is entered while reading history, not while looking at the files it writes. A file with unstaged edits is left alone, because the pre-image cannot be found.

    Only the whole-commit case is confirmed: it can touch every file the commit touched, whereas a single line is a keystroke.

    Reset¶

    Three verbs, separated by how much of the repository each one moves: a reset moves the branch, a detached checkout moves only HEAD, and restoring a file moves neither. All three are entered from a commit, which is the rule rather than a convenience — a menu of verbs would let somebody reach --hard without having a commit in mind.

    --hard is the most destructive thing this app does

    Everything else that writes can be read back off some surface afterwards. A hard reset overwrites the working tree, and that half is in no reflog.

    So the dialog states two things as deliberately separate sentences: the commits leaving the branch stay reachable through the reflog, and the working tree does not. Both halves are load-bearing. Without the first, a reset reads as permanent and nobody does one; without the second, it reads as reversible, which is the misunderstanding the dialog exists to prevent.

    A working-tree backup is taken first, over exactly the files the dialog named, so there is usually a way back after all — and the log row says plainly when there is not.

    The dialog composes none of that itself: the count, the commit subjects, the files about to be overwritten and the untracked ones that will be spared all come from the same plan the operation consults, and a test asserts the refused sentence and the emitted error are the same string.

    An unknown count refuses, in every mode. A confirmation reading "this will discard some number of things" is not one, and a repository whose graph or status will not read is precisely where writing anyway is worst.

    Untracked files are left alone, as git's own reset --hard leaves them — they are in no tree, so no tree can restore them.

    Nothing resets while something else is half-finished, including the replay this app drives, which leaves git's own repository state at NONE and is therefore looked for separately.

    Detaching, and coming back¶

    Checking out a commit detaches HEAD, the banner says so on every surface, and the way back is read out of HEAD's own reflog — the checkout: moving from <branch> to <commit> entry that git checkout - reads too. So a detach performed by git outside the app gets offered the same way back, and there is no second file to keep true. A branch deleted since the detach is not offered at all, because a way back that fails on being taken is worse than none.

    A detach is not a ref move, which is the neatest way to get the undo wrong: no branch moved, and a mixed reset against a detached HEAD drags it back and leaves the repository detached — indistinguishable from having gone back, and the next commit made there is orphaned. That row points at re-attaching instead.

    Restoring one file¶

    Not a reset and not refused like one: nothing about HEAD moves and nothing is committed, so it lands on Changes to be read. Writing over an uncommitted edit to that path is the request rather than an accident, so it goes ahead. A path the commit does not hold is refused — a checkout whose pathspec matches nothing succeeds having written nothing, and "restored" with the file untouched is the worst outcome available.

    Dragging one commit onto another¶

    The gesture every other client has, and the one this project's rules make most dangerous: a drag is ambiguous — merge, rebase and cherry-pick are three different operations over the same pair — and a slip of the hand must never replay a branch.

    So the drop names the operation, and choosing the row is the second gesture. A menu opens at the point the drop landed, spells the pair out at the top, and every row does exactly what the commit menu's row of that name does. The drop itself decides nothing, which is the whole safeguard.

    One end has to be the current branch's commit, because every verb here is relative to HEAD: dragged from it is "move where I am onto there", dropped onto it is "bring that here". Anything else draws a sentence, because a popup that opens with nothing in it reads as broken rather than as a refusal — and the drop wash says, before you let go, whether dropping there would offer anything.

    The row is dragged by a grip and clicked by its body, because the body is already spoken for twice, by the click that selects and the right-click that opens the menu.
