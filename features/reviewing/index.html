    Reviewing a proposal¶

    Licensing — Paid. forge-write, with the held-and-sent-later half offline-comments. What a licence adds

    The one thing in this app that changes something on a machine that is not this one.

    The panel is two panes¶

    A reader is only ever doing one of two things, and everything used to be one column — description, size, reviews, then a lower list with three chips over it — so a proposal of any substance was several screens of scrolling in a panel about 420px wide.

    • Proposal is deciding whether to pick this up: what it says it does, how big it is, and what its reviewers have said.
    • Content is reading what it changes.

    The identity stays above the panes — number, state, title, author, branches, labels, and how long it has been waiting — for the tab bar's own reason: what you are looking at must not disappear when you change which part of it you are looking at.

    Reviews sit with the proposal, because a review is a judgement about a change rather than part of it, and it is what the approval waits are computed from. Content is one pane and not two: commits and files are two readings of the same change, and they nest — a commit chosen out of the list is read inside the commits reading, so promoting the two would put "← Commits" at the same level as the tabs and make "Files" and "the files of the commit I opened" read as two lists that both exist.

    Nothing is fetched by arriving at either pane, and Content rests on Files because the files came with the selection while the commits are still bought by their own chip.

    The diff of somebody else's proposal is a diff¶

    A forge sends a unified patch per file, and it is turned into the same shape the local diff uses — so the same panel draws it, with the same highlighting, the same split and wrap preferences, and the same intra-line marks, none of it written twice.

    Staging, reverting and discarding are all absent: there is no index behind somebody else's branch. And it is deliberately not re-readable, because re-reading matches on the path — so a proposal touching a file also open in Changes would otherwise have the local version silently swapped in the moment a context option changed.

    Three honesty requirements sit on top of it:

    • A patch that did not arrive is not an empty diff. GitHub withholds one for three unrelated reasons — nothing to diff as text, a diff it has and will not send, and a pure rename — and they are told apart, because "no textual changes in this file" is true of all three and therefore says nothing about any.
    • A patch that will not parse yields nothing rather than half a diff, since half a hunk drawn as a whole one is a diff that is quietly wrong about which lines moved.
    • A commit's diff is the commit as it stands on its branch, which after a rebase, or for a merge, is not what the proposal would land. The pane says so, because both readings render as something plausible.

    Whitespace is honoured, and the anchoring is what makes that safe. GitHub's REST API takes no whitespace parameter — the ?w=1 people know is its web UI's — so the filtering is applied to the patch that arrived. Nothing is ever renumbered: a reindented pair collapses to one context line carrying the removal's old number beside the addition's new one, and every surviving line keeps the numbers the forge gave it. That is the load-bearing property, because a comment is anchored to a line number on a side and a filter that closed the numbering up would put somebody's objection on a line nobody wrote. The context dial cannot be honoured at all — no filtering adds back lines that were never sent — and the pane says so.

    Three kinds of comment, and each says which¶

    Who can see it Where it is
    Published Everybody The forge
    Pending Its author alone The forge, in an unsubmitted review — so a browser shows it
    Kept here Nobody outside this app This machine

    The middle one is the kind worth being careful with, because it carries an author and a time exactly as a published one does — drawn without a word saying otherwise it reads as something the proposal's author has already seen, which is the single most consequential thing a reviewer can be wrong about here.

    So the tag is a word and not only a tint, and the two unsent kinds get different words: collapsing them would hide the difference that decides whether closing the app loses anything.

    The count on the band and the per-file comment count are over all three — a file carrying three of your own unsent objections and nothing else read as a file nobody had commented on.

    The review being written lives on the forge¶

    It was the other way round for most of this feature's life, on the argument that a server-side draft makes every comment a round trip, puts a second copy of somebody's half-finished thinking on another machine, and leaves this app reconciling two of them whenever both have moved.

    The last of those decided it, and it decided it the other way: there is one copy now and nothing to reconcile. A comment is appended to the pending review the moment it is written, and the same review is what a browser shows. What is given up is that writing a comment needs the network; what is bought is that a review begun here can be finished anywhere.

    Nothing about the local half was deleted, because losing what somebody typed is the one outcome that is never acceptable: a provider that cannot append, no token, no commit to anchor to, or a request that failed all end with the comment kept here. That is not silent either — the state says failed, the toast says so, and the band says the comment is being kept here and why. A draft that quietly stopped syncing is worse than one that never tried.

    The second read nobody expected¶

    A pending comment is on the forge and in no comment list: the published list is what everybody can see, and a review that has not been submitted is visible to its author alone — the forge keeps its comments under the review's own path and nowhere else.

    While the draft was local there was nothing to miss. The moment it moved, every comment a reviewer wrote became something a browser drew and this app did not: the thread appeared once, in the session that wrote it, and was gone after a relaunch, after "read it again", and entirely for a review begun in a browser.

    So there is a second read, under its own key — a third question rather than a share of the published list's, because the two are read from different endpoints and go stale for different reasons: that one changes when somebody else says something, and this one changes every time you write.

    It costs a request only for the reader who has a review open: the id comes off the review list the app already bought, so a proposal with no pending review asks for nothing extra.

    Writing one¶

    Comment is in the hunk header rather than on every line, because a button per line would be a palette entry and a hint key per line — forty entries reading "Comment" and the alphabet spent on a list that runs to thousands. It acts on the panel's own selection, which is how staging has always worked here, and a forge takes a comment on a range, so two lines picked and one comment written is exactly the shape both ends already have.

    The composer offers two verbs and they are not the same act:

    • Add to review is the primary — it goes into the pending review, and is reversible.
    • Comment now publishes immediately, is offered only where the forge takes one, and is the only write with a way back beside it.

    Rewriting a comment reaches the third kind alone, and the button is absent on the other two. A published comment would need a PATCH, which is absent from the write list on purpose. A pending one is on the forge and rewriting it needs a second GraphQL mutation — the one allow-list row that names no operation, and widening it further to save retyping a sentence is not a trade worth making. A comment kept here is the fallback, so this app is its only holder and nothing leaves the machine — and that is not the marginal case it sounds like: a comment is kept here because sending it failed, which makes it the one most likely to want a word changed before the review is sent again.

    The field opens in place of the rendered body rather than under it, since a box below the same sentence would show it twice and leave you working out which of the two the buttons act on.

    Submitting¶

    Sending a review that was already begun is a different request, and picking the wrong one was the bug. Creating a review out of whatever this app is holding is right when the draft never reached the forge and wrong the moment it did — and it did, in the ordinary case, because that is where comments go now. A forge allows one pending review per person per proposal, so creating a second is a 422: "Unprocessable entity", on every press of Approve after anything had been written.

    The half that was worse than the error is that the request it refused carried none of the comments, which are in the pending review — so succeeding would have been wrong too.

    So there are two verbs and the app picks between them on whether a pending review id answers. The submission sends the verdict and the covering note and nothing else: the comments are already in the review it names, each anchored to the commit it was written against.

    A local draft that still holds comments is the fallback having fired for some of them — those are in no review, so the submission cannot carry them, and they are put straight back into the draft with a notice saying so. A notice rather than an error, because the review was sent and what is left is work still waiting, which is a different piece of news from a failure.

    Only a success discards the draft, because what the forge then holds is the authority and a second copy would be sent twice by the next press. A failed submission keeps what it could not send and says why beside it — every reason a submission fails is one the same draft can be sent under a minute later.

    The band¶

    The worst failure here is losing somebody's review; the second worst is their never noticing it was not sent. So the band is drawn from whether a pending review exists rather than from a tab or a hover, it counts the comments, it says when it was begun, and it says "not sent".

    That predicate is not "is something being kept here": bound to the local draft alone, the band went dark the moment the comments started reaching the forge — which is to say it went dark in the ordinary case, for a review a browser was showing.

    Discarding discards, on both machines¶

    Dropping this app's copy was never the whole of the verb once the review moved: what it left behind was a pending review sitting on somebody's proposal, shown in a browser as unsent work, with nothing here admitting it existed — "discard" meaning "forget", which is the one thing a destructive-sounding verb must not quietly mean.

    The forge's copy goes first and the local one only if it went. Of the two half-failures one is recoverable and the other is not: dropping the local half and failing on the forge loses what somebody typed and leaves the review there; failing and keeping everything loses nothing and can be pressed again.

    So a refusal throws nothing away and the sentence names that the forge still holds it — somebody told only "it failed" would reasonably conclude the review is gone. Where the forge is holding a review it will not take back at all, the local half is dropped and you are told plainly that its copy stands and its own page is where that one has to go: the alternative is a Discard button that cannot be pressed on a review this app itself opened. The confirmation says which of the two cases it is in before it is pressed.

    That DELETE is a widening of the write list and not a crossing of it: what it deletes is a pending review, one this app opened, holding comments only its author can see. The list already takes one of those comments back, and allowing the first while refusing the second would mean a reviewer could take back three comments one at a time and never be rid of the review they were in. What keeps it off a submitted review — or anybody else's — is where the id comes from: the pending state and nothing else.

    Where the draft is kept¶

    A file, and deliberately not the forge cache. That store is a cache: its schema carries a version and a bump throws every row away, which is right for rows whose entire replacement cost is a round trip and catastrophic for the only copy of something a person wrote. And signing out empties it outright — but a half-written review is not a private repository's data leaking onto disk after the account was forgotten, it is the reviewer's own words.

    So signing out keeps the draft, and un-registering the repository is what discards one.

    It is JSON beside the settings rather than in them, because a growing list of records in a settings file is an abuse of the shape, and it is written through on every keystroke: a save deferred to quit is a save that does not happen when the app is killed. Nothing recorded can carry a credential — it is paths, line numbers, a verdict and prose — and a test reads the file's bytes back.

    Two launches used to erase each other's drafts

    The file is one per machine and nothing stops the app being launched twice — a second window made that ordinary rather than unusual. Both copies read it once, held the rows in memory and rewrote the whole thing on every operation, so the second one to write erased everything the other had recorded since it loaded — including the only copy of unsent prose.

    Every mutation re-reads the file immediately before writing now, which collapses the window from a whole session to a single write.

    A 403 is about the scope¶

    Rendering it as "the forge said no" would leave somebody re-typing a review that will be refused again. The sentence is composed from the provider's own scope reasons, so the sign-in panel and the review band cannot come to name different things — and on GitHub the repo scope covers reading and reviewing both, which is exactly why it is said out loud: granting it to read pull requests also grants the ability to approve one in your name.

    Mark as viewed¶

    Unimplemented, which is a smaller and more honest thing to say than what used to stand here. GitHub has a viewed state and exposes it through GraphQL alone, and the reason this app did not reach it was that doing so would put that endpoint on the write allow-list. That row now exists, so the objection has expired rather than been answered.

    The ticks are kept in the draft, and the panel says which: a viewed state that silently fails to sync is worse than none.

    What GitLab and Bitbucket cannot do¶

    Each is a refusal with a sentence rather than an empty list — the rule the whole layer keeps.

    GitLab has no server-side draft: a note posts the instant it is written and there is no container to accumulate comments in. So the local draft is the mechanism there, which is what this app did everywhere before the draft moved onto the forge — a working arrangement rather than a degraded one, and the band already says which of the three kinds you are looking at.

    It also has no way to record an objection as a verdict: its model is approval and un-approval. So "changes requested" posts the covering note and approves nothing, and the entry names what actually happened (commented) rather than what was asked for — un-approving in its place would record a different act under your name.

    Its approvals are read from the events rather than the direct endpoint, because that one answers who has approved right now and carries no timestamps — and the waits reading computes the merge wait from the earliest approval in force, so a wait measured from a missing timestamp is a wait of fifty years.

    A comment there costs two requests and the first is a read: GitLab anchors a note with a position naming three commits, and the trio is read per comment rather than cached, because a merge request that gained a push between two comments has different refs and a stale trio would anchor the second comment to a diff nobody is looking at.

    Bitbucket reads and writes nothing, and that is a decision rather than an unfinished path. Every write worth having there is a modification — leaving a review is a PUT on the proposal, and requesting reviewers or setting labels is a PUT that replaces the set somebody else chose — and a row nothing exercises is a row nobody audits, so shipping an approve row to have a write would be exactly the thing that keeps PUT off the table.

    What it has instead of reviews is participants — a person with a role, an approved flag and a timestamp — which map onto the review fields honestly. A participant who has neither approved nor objected is dropped: being added to a proposal is not reviewing it, and a review list full of people who have said nothing makes "nobody has reviewed this" unanswerable in the one place it is asked.
